# Research Agent 3 — Actual Fighter Physical Needs

Scope: Boxing, Muay Thai, Kickboxing, MMA. Purpose: ground THE PROTOCOL's exercise/programming choices in the real physical demands and common deficiencies of combat athletes, and define what a single shared foundation must and must not try to cover.

Evidence bar: systematic reviews / meta-analyses / peer-reviewed sports science graded [STRONG]; single well-designed studies or consistent primary-study findings graded [MODERATE]; single small studies or narrative reviews graded [WEAK]; UFC Performance Institute practitioner data (not peer-reviewed, large elite sample) and coaching-derived claims graded [PRACTICE-BASED].

---

## Demands By Sport

### Boxing (3-min rounds; amateur 3x3, pro typically 4–12x3)
- Work:rest at the punch-exchange level is intermittent and skill-dependent: elite amateur boxers show an activity:rest ratio around **18:1** vs **9:1** in novices, with novices' ratio degrading round-on-round (16:1 → 8:1 → 6:1) as stoppages increase with fatigue. Elite boxers throw roughly **61–78 punches per round**. [MODERATE — time-motion studies, mostly amateur/novice samples]
- Physiological load is high-intensity intermittent, not steady-state: mean competition heart rate ~192 bpm (peak 178–204 bpm on graded testing) and post-bout blood lactate ~13.6–15 mmol/L. [STRONG — systematic review with meta-analysis, Finlay et al. 2023, k=25 studies]
- VO2max in trained boxers is high: mean ~59.5 ml/kg/min on treadmill testing, with peak VO2 during simulated rounds of ~56–59 ml/kg/min; broader reported range 41–65 ml/kg/min. [MODERATE — multiple primary studies, elite-skewed samples]
- UFC PI cross-sectional data (MMA-specific but useful as an upper-bound performance-norm reference) show striking-derived alactic power (10-sec Wattbike sprint) benchmarks scaling from ~830–1200W (flyweight) to ~1700–1750W (heavyweight) "good" tier, and VO2peak norms of 39.9–57.9 ml/kg/min across weight classes (heavier classes lower). [PRACTICE-BASED — UFC PI Volume 2, n>600 fighters, not peer-reviewed]

### Muay Thai (5x3-min rounds, amateur/pro)
- Effort:pause ratio close to **2:3** at the action level — shorter and more frequent than boxing's exchange pattern, with high-intensity bursts averaging ~2.6s separated by ~11.3s lower-intensity periods. [MODERATE]
- Match heart rate averages **~178.9 bpm**, consistently above anaerobic threshold for the duration of the bout, with blood lactate averaging **~9.7 mmol/L** (lower peak than boxing/kickboxing, consistent with pacing over 5 rounds rather than 3). [MODERATE]
- Fighter VO2max: reported means range ~48.5–54.3 ml/kg/min across studies; a 90-day fight-camp intensified block raised VO2max from 49.3 to 54.8 ml/kg/min (+11%), showing this is trainable, not fixed. [MODERATE, small samples]
- Winners are not consistently more aerobically fit than losers — technical proficiency and pacing/tactical efficiency distinguish outcomes more than raw physiological capacity. [WEAK — single matched-pairs observational study]
- The clinch is a Muay Thai-specific, high-force, low-velocity phase: sustained isometric/near-isometric neck, trunk and hip-drive demand not present at anywhere near the same volume in boxing. [PRACTICE-BASED / WEAK direct evidence, but structurally undeniable given the ruleset]

### Kickboxing (typically 3x3 to 5x3 depending on federation/style: full contact, light contact, point)
- Peak blood lactate in elite K1 competition reported at **~14.6 mmol/L**, not returning to baseline after 20 minutes' recovery — comparable or higher metabolic stress than boxing per bout. [MODERATE]
- Heart rate doesn't differ much by contact style, but full/light-contact styles spend more time in high-intensity HR zones than point fighting; anaerobic metabolism plays a dominant role. [MODERATE]
- Structurally kickboxing sits between boxing (arms-only, longer sustained exchanges) and Muay Thai (added clinch/knees) — legs are loaded far more than in boxing (kicks, checking, sprawling) but without Muay Thai's clinch volume. [PRACTICE-BASED synthesis]

### MMA (non-title 3x5-min rounds; title 5x5-min rounds)
- Work:rest at the action level is **1:2 to 1:4** (work bursts 6–14s, low-intensity/positioning periods 15–36s); factoring in the 1-minute inter-round rest, effective ratio becomes 6:1–9:1. High:low intensity actions run about 2:1. This sits structurally between pure grappling sports (judo/wrestling ~3:1, ~35s work bouts) and pure striking sports (Muay Thai/kickboxing ~2:3–1:2, ~7s work bouts) — MMA is the hybrid demand profile. [MODERATE — time-motion synthesis across several primary studies]
- In-competition blood lactate is markedly higher than in boxing/Muay Thai: simulated sparring and official matches both produce values around **20 mmol/L**, versus resting 0.7–1.0 mmol/L. [MODERATE]
- Aerobic contribution across an entire bout is estimated at **62–86%** of total energy expenditure (comparable combat-sport data from boxing/karate/taekwondo/judo/fencing), even though the decisive actions (strikes, takedowns, scrambles) are anaerobically fuelled — repeatability of those actions depends on aerobic capacity/recovery between bursts. [MODERATE, extrapolated across sports rather than MMA-specific measurement]
- Fight position distribution (UFC 2017–2019, n=1,443 bouts): ~60–62% of fight time at striking distance, ~15% in clinch (mostly against the fence), remainder on the ground (guard, half guard, back/side control, mount, misc.) — distance striking dominates time, but clinch and ground phases are frequent enough to be structurally required, not occasional. [STRONG — UFC PI Volume 2, large official-bout dataset, not independently peer-reviewed but high N and low bias]
- Endurance decline in round 3 has been associated with up to an **18% increase in strikes absorbed**. Striking *skill* itself doesn't appear to degrade much with fatigue in some analyses, but reaction time/consistency and the ability to convert output into accurate strikes does. [WEAK-MODERATE — mixed evidence, smaller N studies]

### Cross-sport physiological comparison (round length effect)
- Shorter rounds (boxing, 3 min) permit higher peak intensity per round and a steeper work:rest ratio (more work, less rest) because the round itself imposes a hard reset; blood lactate spikes are high but pacing across only 3–12 rounds is different from a 5x3 or 5x5 format.
- Longer round-count formats (Muay Thai 5x3, MMA up to 5x5) force more deliberate pacing — evidenced by Muay Thai's lower peak lactate than boxing/kickboxing despite comparable or greater total work, and by MMA fighters visibly moderating output in rounds 1–2 to preserve output for the back half.
- All four sports converge on the same underlying physiological signature: high, sustained heart rate (75–95%+ of max for the duration of engagement), high peak blood lactate (9–20+ mmol/L depending on sport/format), and VO2max requirements in the 40–60 ml/kg/min range that scale down as body mass/weight class increases. This convergence is the strongest evidence for a shared aerobic-plus-repeat-power foundation across all four sports.

## Force Production

- **Punch force is predominantly a lower-body and trunk event, not an arm event.** Consistent estimates: arm/shoulder musculature contributes ~24–25% of punch force; trunk rotation contributes ~37%; legs/lower-limb drive contribute ~39–40% (or described elsewhere as "60–70% of punching force comes from lower-limb drive" when trunk+leg are combined against arm alone). [MODERATE — multiple biomechanics studies converge on this proportion, though exact splits vary by punch type and study]
- Kinetic chain sequencing for a punch: ground reaction force initiated at the foot/ankle → knee → hip rotation → trunk rotation → shoulder → arm extension → fist, with the hip described as the "bridge" linking lower and upper body force transfer. Efficient sequencing (not just raw segment strength) determines how much of that force reaches the target. [MODERATE — kinematic/kinetic analyses, boxing-specific]
- Fatigue disproportionately degrades lower-limb contribution to punching before it degrades everything else: rear-leg drive and lower-limb force generation are specifically implicated as the first casualties of fatigue-driven punch-force decline, consistent with the round-by-round striking decline data below. [MODERATE]
- **Kicking force** (roundhouse kick, comparable across Muay Thai/karate/taekwondo): driven by rapid pelvic axial rotation, hip abduction, hip flexion and knee extension velocity, plus whole-body center-of-mass movement toward the target. Muay Thai practitioners specifically show shorter execution time (~1.02s vs ~1.54s in taekwondo) and the highest foot velocity at impact (~7.22 m/s vs ~5.57–6.36 m/s in karate/taekwondo) despite lower knee-extension velocity than the other two styles — i.e., Muay Thai's roundhouse relies more on hip/pelvis rotation and total-body mass transfer than pure limb-segment speed. [MODERATE — comparative biomechanics study, n small]
- Punching-power training implication: strength training alone (getting stronger, without deliberately training speed) reliably increases power output in trained boxers; combined strength+speed training produces larger gains than either alone. A 6-week core-strength intervention alone produced a 24% increase in rear-hand punch impact power in professional boxers. [MODERATE-STRONG — RCT-level and systematic-review evidence]
- Upper-body power asymmetry between dominant/non-dominant arms is common and often exceeds 15–20% in UFC-tested fighters (e.g., flyweight ~28.6% asymmetry, welterweight ~17.8%), suggesting unilateral, not just bilateral, power development is relevant to injury-resilience and output consistency. [PRACTICE-BASED — UFC PI Volume 2]

## Repeat-Effort Ability and Fatigue: What Degrades First

Ordered roughly from earliest/most consistently observed to latest/least consistently observed across the literature reviewed:

1. **Mechanical punch/strike intensity (force and velocity) declines progressively across rounds**, with the sharpest drop after round 1 and then a more gradual decline/stabilization; weaker punch types degrade more than an athlete's strongest punch (–9.84% vs –4.63% in one fatigue protocol). [MODERATE]
2. **Lower-limb force contribution to strikes degrades before upper-body technique fully breaks down** — rear-leg drive is specifically flagged as fatigue-vulnerable, meaning power output falls even when the "shape" of the technique still looks correct. [MODERATE]
3. **Output volume and repeatability (repeat-effort ability) decline** — this is functionally the round:round decline seen in boxing work:rest ratios (novices' work:rest collapsing from 16:1 to 6:1) and MMA's round-3 fatigue effect (up to 18% increase in strikes absorbed). [MODERATE]
4. **Reaction time and reaction consistency degrade under fatigue**, which functionally undermines defense and counter-timing even before gross technique collapses; this is documented directly in MMA fighters using cognitive-reaction protocols under fatigue. [MODERATE — PLOS ONE study, MMA-specific]
5. **Decision-making/tactical judgment is the most fatigue-vulnerable layer** in principle (broader sports-science consensus that mental fatigue impairs decision performance under fatigue, generalized from adjacent sports since direct MMA/boxing decision-making-under-fatigue data is thinner), but evidence directly measuring in-competition decision quality decline in boxing/Muay Thai/kickboxing specifically is comparatively weak. [WEAK — mostly extrapolated from adjacent domains]
6. Notably, **pure technical skill/technique execution appears to hold up better than expected** in at least one MMA analysis — fighters in longer fights didn't show reduced skill level despite fatigue, suggesting that well-grooved technique is fatigue-resistant relative to force output, accuracy-at-volume, and decision speed. [WEAK — single study, contradicts naive "everything falls apart" assumption and is worth flagging as a genuine uncertainty]

Bottom line for programming: fatigue attacks **force and repeatability of force** first and most reliably; it attacks **reaction speed/decision quality** meaningfully but with less direct evidence; it may leave **rehearsed technical shape** comparatively intact longer than assumed. This argues for prioritizing repeat-power and aerobic-recovery-between-bursts training over pure "cardio" in the generic sense.

## Injury & Protective Qualities

| Sport | Most-injured regions (competition) | Most-injured regions (training) | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Boxing | Head/face (median 72% of comp injuries; contusions dominant) | Upper limb (median 49%; muscle strains dominant) | Shoulder injuries ~2x more common in amateurs (9%) than professionals (4%); supraspinatus most-affected rotator cuff muscle (~39% positive on Drop Arm Test in the reviewed sample). Wrist (~12%) and lower back (~7.8%) also notable. [STRONG — systematic review + meta-analysis for overall rates; MODERATE for shoulder-specific breakdown] |
| Muay Thai / Kickboxing | Extremities (~58% of injuries); lower limb fractures more common than in boxing | Combat simulation/sparring (43% injury prevalence) > technical training (26%) > competition (10%) | Head trauma reported 5–66% depending on study/ruleset; self-reported concussion lower (~5.4%). Ankle sprains common from checking/kicking mechanics; shin/foot overuse from repeated kicking. [MODERATE] |
| MMA (extrapolated partly from adjacent grappling sports, direct MMA systematic reviews are sparse) | Knee most common single site (~9.7%, judo-derived comparator data); shoulder and lower back also high (~10.2%, ~10.9% in judo) | Strains/sprains most common injury type overall (~31.7%), then fractures (~18.8%), then contusions/abrasions (~20.2%) | Direct high-quality MMA-specific injury epidemiology is limited; much of the best evidence is imported from judo/BJJ/wrestling as structurally similar grappling-inclusive combat sports. This is a real evidence gap, flagged below. [WEAK-MODERATE, evidence-quality caveat explicit] |

Protective qualities with direct evidence:
- **Neck strength is protective against concussion.** In the strongest single dataset reviewed (high-school athletes, cross-sport), each 1-lb increase in neck strength reduced concussion odds by ~5%; smaller neck circumference/neck-to-head ratio and weaker overall neck strength were independently associated with concussion risk. Evidence is strongest in adjacent contact sports (football, soccer) rather than boxing/MMA specifically, but the mechanism (reduced head acceleration under impact) generalizes directly to striking sports. [STRONG mechanism, MODERATE direct combat-sport evidence]
- **Hip abduction strength and hip mobility deficits are common and correctable.** A cohort of elite adolescent athletes found roughly half had marked hip-abduction weakness or >25% side-to-side asymmetry at baseline; 8 weeks of targeted training resolved this in the large majority. Amateur athletes with chronic low back pain specifically show limited internal hip rotation and reduced muscular endurance. This directly implicates hip mobility/strength as both a low-back-pain risk factor and a correctable gap. [MODERATE]
- **Shin conditioning and correct kick mechanics (striking with shin, not foot) reduce ankle and foot injury risk in kicking sports**; checking kicks correctly (not with foot/ankle exposed) is the primary technical protective factor against acute ankle injury from low kicks. [PRACTICE-BASED / WEAK formal evidence, but near-universal in coaching literature]

## Common Weaknesses In Non-Elite Fighters

Synthesizing the injury-risk-factor literature, the strength-training-transfer literature, and general and combat-sport athletic-development literature:

- **Hip mobility and hip abduction/rotation strength** are disproportionately underdeveloped, show up as correctable within 6–8 weeks of targeted work, and sit upstream of both kicking power (roundhouse mechanics are hip-rotation-driven) and low-back pain (limited internal hip rotation is specifically associated with chronic LBP in amateur athletes).
- **Rear-leg/lower-body force contribution to striking** is an easy area to under-train relative to upper-body "hitting the bag" work, yet it supplies the majority of punch force and is also the first thing fatigue erodes — meaning undertrained legs compound under fatigue rather than just underperforming at baseline.
- **Neck strength** is rarely trained directly by recreational/amateur fighters despite being one of the few qualities with strong protective evidence against a serious, sport-relevant injury (concussion), and is structurally required for the Muay Thai/MMA clinch.
- **Aerobic capacity/repeatability** is inconsistent in amateurs: novice work:rest ratios collapse round-on-round (16:1→6:1) in a way elite boxers' don't, indicating amateurs are frequently undertrained in the specific "repeat high-intensity effort with incomplete recovery" quality rather than generic steady-state cardio.
- **Core/trunk rotational strength** is commonly undertrained relative to its demonstrated contribution (~37% of punch force from trunk rotation alone; a 6-week core program alone produced 24% more punch power in trained boxers) — it's a comparatively cheap, high-yield gap.
- **Grip strength and grip endurance** are specifically underdeveloped in strikers moving toward MMA/clinch-heavy styles; grappling-sport data shows grip strength can drop 80–90% within a single match without specific conditioning, and it correlates with grappling performance.
- **Shoulder health/rotator cuff capacity** is a known weak point disproportionately affecting amateurs over professionals in boxing (9% vs 4% injury rate), suggesting technique/volume management plus targeted rotator-cuff/scapular strength work is under-prioritized at the amateur level.

## Qualities Ranked By Transfer

Based on the combined strength-training-transfer systematic reviews, force-production biomechanics, and physiological-demand data reviewed, roughly ordered by strength and consistency of evidence for transfer to combat performance:

1. **Maximal strength (particularly lower-body and trunk)** — most consistent evidence: higher strength levels correlate with higher competitive level and greater punching force; strength gains transfer to power even without dedicated speed work. [STRONG-MODERATE]
2. **Rotational/trunk (core) strength and power** — direct causal evidence via intervention studies (24% punch-power increase from an isolated 6-week core program); also the single largest non-leg contributor to punch force (~37%). [MODERATE-STRONG]
3. **Repeat-effort/anaerobic-alactic power (ability to reproduce near-maximal short efforts with incomplete recovery)** — matches the actual competition demand pattern (work bursts of 2–14s depending on sport) better than either pure max strength or pure aerobic conditioning alone; UFC PI treats this as a core testable quality (10x6s repeat-sprint protocol) precisely because it differentiates fighters. [MODERATE, PRACTICE-BASED for the specific testing protocol]
4. **Aerobic capacity (VO2max) as a recovery/repeatability enabler, not a peak-output driver** — the evidence is that aerobic system underpins the *ability to repeat* anaerobic bursts and recover between rounds (62–86% of total energy expenditure across a bout is aerobic) rather than driving peak force or velocity itself. Evidence for aerobic fitness directly predicting fight outcome is comparatively weaker and mixed (Muay Thai winners weren't more aerobically fit than losers in one study). [MODERATE for the physiological role; WEAK-MODERATE for direct outcome prediction]
5. **Grip strength/endurance** — strong and specific evidence in grappling contexts (judo/wrestling), directly relevant to MMA and Muay Thai clinch, essentially irrelevant to boxing. [MODERATE, sport-conditional]
6. **Neck strength** — strong protective-injury evidence (concussion risk reduction), plausible but less-directly-evidenced performance contribution (clinch stability, head stabilization for target acquisition while striking). [STRONG for injury protection, WEAK-MODERATE for direct performance transfer]
7. **General flexibility/mobility (non-hip)** — weakest evidence tier reviewed; hip mobility specifically is well-evidenced (see above) but general flexibility as a standalone performance predictor is not well supported by the sources found in this review. [WEAK]

## Shared Core vs Sport-Specific Divergences

**Shared core (applies to boxer, Muay Thai fighter, kickboxer, and MMA athlete alike — and should be the large majority of the product):**
- High-intensity repeat-effort capacity (short maximal bursts, incomplete recovery) — the physiological signature converges across all four sports (HR 75-95%+ of max sustained, blood lactate 9-20+ mmol/L, VO2max needs 40-60 ml/kg/min scaling inversely with weight class).
- Lower-body and hip-driven force production for striking (legs+trunk supply ~75% of punch force in every one of these sports; hip rotation drives kicks in three of the four).
- Trunk/core rotational strength (same ~37% contribution logic; directly trainable with high ROI evidence).
- General maximal strength as a power-output base (strength-transfers-to-power evidence is sport-agnostic).
- Neck strength (protective across all striking exposure, and structurally required in the two sports with clinch).
- Fatigue-resistant repeat-power training specifically targeting the legs (since leg-drive is what degrades first under fatigue across the board).
- Shoulder health/rotator cuff resilience (elevated injury risk in the amateur population broadly, not sport-specific).
- Hip mobility and hip-rotation strength (upstream of both kicking-sport force production and low-back-pain risk even in non-kicking boxing).

**Sport-specific divergences (small in number, but must be explicitly acknowledged rather than smoothed away):**
1. **Kicking-specific hip/pelvis rotational speed and lower-limb range of motion** — required in Muay Thai/kickboxing/MMA, essentially absent in boxing. A boxer doesn't need hip-abduction ROM for a roundhouse kick; a Muay Thai fighter's performance is bottlenecked by it.
2. **Clinch-specific isometric strength and grip/neck endurance** — required in Muay Thai and MMA (and present in a different, ground-based form in MMA's wrestling/BJJ phases), structurally absent from boxing and largely absent from kickboxing rulesets that don't permit clinch.
3. **Grappling/ground repeat-effort and grip endurance** — MMA-specific among these four (takedowns, scrambles, ground control are ~15%+ clinch and additional ground-position time per the UFC PI positional data); irrelevant to pure striking sports.
4. **Round-count/pacing profile** — boxing's 3-minute rounds reward higher per-round peak output with a harder reset; Muay Thai/MMA's longer formats (5x3, up to 5x5) reward pacing and aerobic-based recovery between bursts more heavily. This is a programming/periodization divergence more than a "different muscle qualities" divergence.
5. **Lower-limb impact tolerance (shin/ankle) for kicking sports** — Muay Thai/kickboxing/MMA fighters need shin/ankle load tolerance (checking kicks, kicking mechanics) that boxers structurally don't.

## Uncertainties

- **MMA-specific injury epidemiology is thinner than boxing/Muay Thai's.** Much of the MMA injury-region data above leans on judo/BJJ/wrestling as structural proxies for MMA's grappling phases; a dedicated, large-sample, prospective MMA injury systematic review comparable to the boxing meta-analysis was not surfaced in this pass and may not yet exist at the same evidence tier.
- **Whether decision-making/tactical judgment genuinely degrades under fight fatigue in these specific sports is not well-established directly** — the strongest evidence found was adjacent (marksmanship, soccer penalties) rather than boxing/MMA-specific; the one MMA-specific finding on skill-under-fatigue actually cuts the other way (skill held up despite fatigue in one dataset). Treat the "decision-making degrades" claim as plausible-by-extrapolation, not confirmed.
- **The UFC PI Volume 2 data is elite-professional and MMA-specific**, not necessarily representative of boxing/Muay Thai/kickboxing athletes or of amateur/recreational populations, which is exactly the population THE PROTOCOL is likely to serve. It was used here as a high-quality performance-norms reference (large N, real competitive population) rather than as direct evidence about non-elite or non-MMA athletes — that gap should be treated as a genuine limitation, not papered over.
- **Muay Thai's "winners weren't more fit than losers" finding is a single small study** and shouldn't be over-weighted against the broader physiological-demand literature; it's included because it's a real, specific finding that complicates a naive "more VO2max = better fighter" narrative, but it is not strong enough to override the aerobic-capacity-as-repeatability-enabler conclusion above.
- **Exact force-contribution percentages for punching (24/37/39% arm/trunk/leg split) vary somewhat by source and punch type** (jab vs cross vs hook behave differently kinetically); treat the specific numbers as illustrative of "legs and trunk dominate" rather than as a precise, universally fixed ratio.

## What This Means For The Product

THE PROTOCOL's foundation should be built almost entirely around the shared core, because the evidence shows the four sports converge much more than they diverge: all four demand repeat high-intensity effort with incomplete recovery, all four load the legs and trunk as the primary force generators for offense, all four show the same fatigue signature (force/output degrades before rehearsed technique fully collapses), and all four share overlapping injury-risk profiles (shoulder, low back, hip, neck) that respond to the same handful of correctable weaknesses (hip mobility/strength, trunk rotational power, neck strength, posterior-chain/leg-drive capacity, shoulder resilience).

Concretely, the foundation program should prioritize, in this order of evidence strength: (1) lower-body and trunk maximal strength and power as the base, because it's the most consistently evidenced transfer pathway and because legs+trunk supply the majority of striking force in every one of these sports; (2) repeat-effort/interval conditioning that mimics real work:rest patterns (short maximal bursts, incomplete recovery — not steady-state cardio) because that's what the time-motion data actually shows fighters doing, in every sport, not just MMA; (3) rotational core strength specifically, because it's cheap to train, has direct causal (not just correlational) evidence of transfer, and underpins both punching and kicking power; (4) neck strength, because it's rarely trained by non-elite athletes despite being one of the few qualities with genuinely strong injury-protective evidence, and it's needed structurally the moment clinch work enters the picture; (5) hip mobility/rotational capacity and shoulder health work as the injury-prevention layer, because both are well-evidenced as commonly deficient and correctable in weeks, not months.

The sport-specific divergences are real but narrow and should be handled as small, clearly-labeled branches off the shared trunk rather than separate programs: a kicking-sport module (hip/pelvis rotational ROM and speed, shin/ankle load tolerance) for Muay Thai/kickboxing/MMA users that a pure boxer can skip without loss; a clinch/grappling module (isometric neck/grip/hip-drive endurance) for Muay Thai/MMA users that a boxer or point-kickboxer doesn't need; and a pacing/periodization distinction between boxing's shorter, harder-reset round structure and the longer, more pacing-dependent formats of Muay Thai and MMA. Framed this way, a single product can genuinely serve all four sports — the risk of "so generic it's useless" is avoidable specifically because the physiological convergence across these sports is not a marketing simplification, it's what the time-motion and physiological-demand data actually show.
