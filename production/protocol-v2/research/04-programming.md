# THE PROTOCOL — Programming & Progression Research

Scope: the simplest programming system that still produces real adaptation for an athlete already doing 3-6 combat sessions/week, self-coached from a phone, no coach, no 1RM testing, no wearables assumed.

**Minimum effective dose, overall:** the literature is consistent that resistance-trained and untrained people alike get real strength gains from as little as 1 hard set, 1-3x/week, per movement, taken close to failure. This is the single most load-bearing finding for a system built around athletes who already have 3-6 combat sessions eating their recovery budget — volume is not the lever to pull; effort and consistency are.

## The Simplest Defensible Progression Rule

**One sentence:** When you can complete the top of your rep range for all prescribed sets with 1-2 reps left in the tank, add reps until you hit the top of the range, then add difficulty (harder variation, added unilateral load, or reduced rest) and drop back to the bottom of the range.

This is **double progression** (reps first, then load/difficulty), and it is the most robust model for a self-coached, no-equipment population for three converging reasons:

1. **It doesn't require RPE precision.** [MODERATE-WEAK evidence for raw RPE/RIR accuracy in novices] — untrained lifters are measurably worse than trained lifters at estimating reps-in-reserve (Zourdos-style studies), and some data show novices can mistake a 2-RIR set for a true max effort. Double progression sidesteps this: the athlete doesn't have to *rate* effort, they just have to *count reps* and notice whether the last 1-2 reps got hard. Rep-counting is a task novices are already accurate at; effort-rating is not.
2. **It's self-limiting and auto-deloads.** Because progression only occurs after a demonstrated buffer (1-2 reps left, not grinding to failure), the model naturally avoids the failure-training fatigue cost — pooled meta-analytic evidence shows training to failure produces no extra strength/hypertrophy over stopping short, while generating materially more fatigue and recovery cost. For an athlete who also spars, this matters more than for a general lifter.
3. **It generalizes to bodyweight without new logic.** For loaded barbell work, "double progression" means reps then external load. For bodyweight-only work, the load axis becomes: harder leverage/variation → unilateral shift → reduced rest/increased density → added tempo (slow eccentric). All four are well-supported individually (see below) and slot into the same "reps up, then bump difficulty, reps back down" loop without the athlete needing a percentage-based system or a 1RM.

**The "leave 2-3 in the tank" heuristic**, used only as a rough guardrail (not the primary progression trigger), is evidence-supported at a practice-based level: an RPE-adjacent cue like "if every rep looked and felt the same speed, you had reps left; if the last rep visibly slowed down, you were close" is more decodable by a beginner than a numeric RPE scale, and matches the coaching literature's own workaround for novice inaccuracy (have novices practice rep-speed cues rather than trust their numeric self-rating early on).

## Regression Rules

Every exercise in the system should have a built-in "down" ladder so a fatigued, sore, or injured athlete always has a legal fallback rather than skipping the session entirely:

- **Reduce range of motion / leverage difficulty** — e.g., pistol squat → box pistol → split squat → goblet-tempo squat. [PRACTICE-BASED, but consistent across all unilateral progression sources reviewed: the leverage/support ladder is the standard method, e.g., split squat regressed to step-up, RFESS regressed further with hand support.]
- **Bilateral instead of unilateral** — drop the single-leg/single-arm demand first; it's the highest-leverage regression because unilateral work compounds both a strength and a stability/balance demand.
- **Reduce reps below the prescribed range, keep sets** — better than skipping the movement pattern.
- **Increase rest, reduce density** — for conditioning-style work, more rest between rounds is the correct regression before reducing round count.
- **Slow the tempo down / add support (wall, chair, TRX-style anchor)** — reduces force and balance demand simultaneously.
- **Swap the modality, not the pattern** — e.g., jump squat → squat (remove the plyometric component but keep the movement) is a legitimate power-to-strength regression when an athlete is too fatigued for real ballistic intent (this matters directly for post-sparring days: ballistic work has the strictest "walk away if not fresh" rule of anything in the system, since sub-maximal-velocity "power" work just becomes junk volume).

## Concurrent Training & Interference

[STRONG, updated understanding] The classic "cardio kills your gains" interference effect is now understood to be **largely overstated for strength and hypertrophy** outcomes, and **real but small** for muscle fiber-type-specific hypertrophy, and **most consistently real** for power/explosive output specifically.

Key findings:
- Schumann et al. (43 studies): concurrent training with proper session sequencing did not meaningfully interfere with hypertrophy or maximal strength, regardless of aerobic modality, frequency, or training status. [STRONG]
- Interference is more pronounced when the endurance modality involves eccentric loading (running > cycling) due to muscle damage overlap. [MODERATE] — directly relevant, since combat training itself (footwork, sparring, bag rounds) is running-like/eccentric-loaded, not cycling-like.
- Interference on power/explosive adaptations is the most robust and consistent finding across reviews — this is the one domain where sequencing and separation genuinely matter. [STRONG-MODERATE]
- **Molecular/physiological basis for spacing:** AMPK signaling from endurance-type work takes at least ~3 hours to return toward baseline; the endurance-then-strength sequence needs roughly a 3-hour gap to avoid blunting the strength-training signal. [MODERATE, mechanistic evidence, extrapolated to applied recommendation]
- **Applied recommendation converging across sources:** if same-day, put the higher-priority/more neurally-demanding session first, and separate sessions by **at least 3 hours**, with **6+ hours** cited as a more conservative, commonly-used buffer in applied settings. Where possible, keep resistance/power training on separate days from hard endurance/interval work, or ≥24h apart, to protect power-specific adaptations. [MODERATE]

**Practical translation for this population:** since combat training (bag work, pad work, sparring) already functions as the "endurance/anaerobic" side of concurrent training, S&C should be sequenced either well before a hard combat session (≥3h, same day) or on separate days entirely — never squeezed in immediately after a hard sparring or interval-heavy combat session, and power/ballistic S&C work specifically should never follow combat training same-day.

## Fatigue Management Around Sparring

This is the highest-stakes scheduling constraint in the whole system, because sparring uniquely combines high neuromuscular demand, eccentric/impact loading, and (unlike a solo S&C session) unpredictable intensity the athlete doesn't fully control.

- **Time course of eccentric/high-intensity damage:** [STRONG] DOMS peaks 24-48h post-exercise (inverted-U curve), inflammatory markers (CRP, creatine kinase) peak in the same 24-48h window, and structural micro-damage (Z-disk disruption) can remain elevated up to 6-8 days. Strength/force output loss is greatest immediately post-exercise and recovers gradually over the following 48h+.
- **Plyometric/neuromuscular-specific recovery:** [MODERATE-STRONG] Acute peripheral (twitch-level) fatigue from high-volume plyometric-type work largely resolves within ~2 hours, but *central* neuromuscular inhibition and jump/explosive performance can remain suppressed for **2-7 days** after a genuinely high-volume stretch-shortening-cycle bout. Applied guidance converges on **48-72h between high-intensity plyometric/power sessions**.
- **Direct implication for this product:** hard sparring should be treated like a high-intensity eccentric/plyometric bout for scheduling purposes. Power and heavy/near-failure strength work should be avoided for **at least 24h after** hard sparring (practical minimum) and ideally the full **48-72h** if the sparring was genuinely hard (competition-intensity, not light technical rounds). Symmetrically, power/ballistic work should not be placed in the 3-6h immediately before a hard sparring session, since it will pre-fatigue the same neural/explosive qualities sparring depends on — this mirrors the "power work needs to be fresh" principle from the rest-interval literature.
- **Ordering principle within a day that contains both combat work and S&C:** if S&C must land same-day as combat training, sequence it either (a) well before combat training with a substantial gap, or (b) as low-intensity/mobility work after combat training — never as heavy strength or power work in the hours immediately following a hard technical/sparring session.
- Evidence is [MODERATE] here rather than [STRONG] specifically for combat sports — most underlying data comes from general eccentric-exercise and plyometric-training literature, extrapolated to sparring; direct sparring-recovery dosing studies are sparse and mostly describe physiological load equivalence between sparring and competition rather than prescriptive recovery windows.

## Weekly Templates (3 / 4 / 5-Day)

Assumes the athlete already has 3-6 combat sessions/week (technical, bag, pad, conditioning) and 1-2 sparring sessions/week scheduled outside this system. These are the **S&C session days only**, placed around a typical combat-heavy week. General rule embedded throughout: never place ballistic/power or max-effort strength work within 24h after hard sparring, and prefer placing power work same-day as combat work only with a same-day gap of 3h+, done before combat training when possible.

**3-day template** (for an athlete already doing 4-5 combat sessions + 1-2 sparring): 1x Strength (full body), 1x Power/Speed (short, low-fatigue), 1x Conditioning/Mobility hybrid. Placed on the athlete's lightest combat days, at least one full day removed from sparring.

- Day A — Strength (full body, 30-40 min): squat pattern, hinge pattern, push, pull, carry/core. Double progression, 2-3 sets each.
- Day B — Power + Speed (20-25 min, short and fresh): jumps/throws, footwork bursts, full recovery between reps. Placed on a day with no sparring for 24h in either direction if possible.
- Day C — Conditioning (anaerobic/repeat-effort, 15-20 min) + Mobility (10 min). Can sit closer to a harder combat day since it's not neurally maximal.

**4-day template**: split strength into upper/lower emphasis to allow slightly higher per-session focus while keeping each session short.

- Day A — Lower Strength
- Day B — Power/Speed (fresh day)
- Day C — Upper Strength
- Day D — Conditioning + Mobility

**5-day template** (higher-volume combat week, athlete wants daily "something"): keep individual sessions very short (15-25 min) and use the extra days for mobility/recovery-oriented work rather than adding real fatigue.

- Day A — Lower Strength
- Day B — Power/Speed
- Day C — Upper Strength
- Day D — Conditioning (anaerobic/repeat-effort)
- Day E — Mobility + light aerobic (recovery-oriented, low fatigue cost, can be placed the day after or before sparring)

In all templates: if a sparring session is added or moved, the rule is "power/strength moves away from sparring, mobility/aerobic can move toward it." No template should place power/ballistic work on the same day as, or the day immediately following, a hard sparring session.

## Monitoring

**Simplest defensible option for a phone-only, no-wearable user: daily 1-5 wellness self-rating (sleep quality, soreness, fatigue, stress/mood) + session RPE x duration ("sRPE load") after each session.** [STRONG for sRPE validity, MODERATE-STRONG for wellness questionnaires]

- Session-RPE (Foster method: RPE 0-10 x session duration in minutes = arbitrary load units) has good validated reliability across sports, ages, and training levels, is dead simple to implement (single number + a timer), and needs no equipment. It's the best-evidenced load metric available without a wearable.
- Simple wellness questionnaires (1-5 scale across sleep, soreness, fatigue, stress, mood, ~5 questions, <1 min to complete) are shown to meaningfully predict readiness and have been used to trigger real coaching adjustments in applied settings (e.g., sprinter cohort study showing next-day improvement after load was adjusted off questionnaire responses).
- Acute:Chronic Workload Ratio (7-day acute load / 28-day chronic load, "sweet spot" 0.8-1.3) is well-known in team-sport injury literature but is now under real scientific dispute for its predictive validity, and it requires enough historical data (~4 weeks) to compute meaningfully — too heavy and too fragile for a v1 self-coached phone product. **Recommend NOT building ACWR into the initial product**; a simple week-over-week "did total sRPE load jump more than ~30-50%" flag captures most of the same signal without the disputed math.
- **Recommended minimum viable monitoring stack for the product:** post-session sRPE (2 numbers: RPE 0-10, duration) + a once-daily 3-question morning check (sleep quality, soreness, motivation/fatigue, each 1-5). This is defensible, cheap to build, and matches what the evidence actually supports as reliable without a coach or wearable.

## Uncertainties

- **Deload necessity at this specific volume/training-age combination has no direct RCT evidence.** No study found tests "combat athlete doing 3-6 sessions/week + minimal-dose S&C, with vs without scheduled deload." The practice-based consensus (novices/recreational lifters training inconsistently don't need scheduled deloads because life already deloads them) is reasonable but not directly evidenced for this exact population. **Given this population already carries a heavy combat-training background load, treat "no separate deload needed" as a working assumption, not a settled finding** — the product should still let the wellness/sRPE monitoring naturally trigger a lighter week rather than assuming volume is always low enough to never need one.
- **Direct sparring-recovery dosing studies are sparse.** Most of the fatigue-management section extrapolates from general eccentric-exercise and plyometric literature rather than sparring-specific trials. The 24h-minimum / 48-72h-ideal buffer is a reasonable synthesis, not a directly-measured number.
- **RIR/RPE accuracy in novices is genuinely mixed in the literature** — one recent study found no experience-related difference in back-squat RIR accuracy, contradicting older findings. This is why the product's progression rule is built around double progression (rep-counting) rather than RPE as the primary trigger, using RPE-style cues only as a soft guardrail.
- **Bodyweight-only strength/hypertrophy evidence is thinner for lower body than upper body** — good evidence exists for push-ups/pull-ups producing real strength gains, less direct evidence for bodyweight-only unilateral leg work matched against loaded training.
- **ACWR's predictive validity is actively disputed in current sports-science literature**, which is why it's explicitly recommended against for v1 despite being the most "official-sounding" monitoring tool.

## What This Means For The Product

1. **Progression logic should be built around double progression (reps → difficulty), not RPE-driven load prescription.** Ask the user to count reps and note whether the last 1-2 reps slowed down — never ask for a numeric RPE/RIR rating as the primary input, since novice accuracy on that specific task is unreliable. Use "did the last reps look/feel different in speed" as the qualitative backstop.
2. **Volume should default low.** 1-3 hard sets per movement is defensible and, more importantly, is the correct choice given this athlete's recovery budget is already spent on combat training — the product should resist the temptation to add "more sets" as its main lever for perceived rigor.
3. **Power/ballistic work needs the strictest scheduling logic in the whole system**: full rest (2-4+ min) within a session, and hard exclusion from the 24h window after sparring and same-day proximity to hard combat sessions. This is the one place where the product should be opinionated and inflexible even if it costs convenience.
4. **Session length should default to 15-40 minutes** — nothing in the adherence literature supports longer sessions producing better completion rates for a self-coached population already training frequently elsewhere; shorter, completable sessions protect adherence more than volume protects adaptation at this dose.
5. **Monitoring should ship as two cheap inputs**: post-session RPE x duration, and a 3-question daily wellness check. Do not build ACWR or anything requiring 4 weeks of historical data before it's useful — it will feel broken during onboarding and its validity is contested anyway.
6. **The weekly template engine's core rule can be expressed simply**: place strength/power S&C on the athlete's freshest days (≥24h from hard sparring in both directions where possible), and let conditioning/mobility absorb the schedule's rough edges, since those are the qualities most tolerant of being placed near fatigue.
7. **No deload logic needs to be hardcoded into week 1-4** of the initial progression; instead, let the wellness/sRPE signal trigger a "back off" recommendation reactively. This keeps the v1 system simple and matches the (admittedly thin) evidence that scheduled deloads matter less at this training volume than at high-volume, high-training-age loads.
