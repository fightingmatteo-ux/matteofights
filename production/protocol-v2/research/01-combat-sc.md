# Research Agent 1 — Combat Sports Strength & Conditioning
## Evidence base for THE PROTOCOL (Power, Speed, Conditioning, Footwork, Strength, Mobility)

Research method: 25 web searches/fetches against peer-reviewed sports science (JSCR, Sports Medicine, IJSPP, Frontiers, MDPI Applied Sciences/IJERPH), systematic reviews/meta-analyses on combat-sport athletes specifically, and named S&C coaches with public evidence-based track records (Boxing Science, UFC PI). Fitness-influencer content was excluded except where explicitly noted as a rejected claim.

---

## Key Findings

### 1. Maximal strength → striking force transfer
Lower-body maximal strength and explosive strength (both upper and lower body) are associated with punch impact force in elite amateur boxers; boxers who punch harder have measurably greater lower-body max strength and explosive strength than lower-impact boxers. **Upper-body maximal strength alone does NOT differentiate high- vs low-impact punchers** — this is the single most counter-intuitive and best-supported finding in the literature. [MODERATE — consistent across multiple primary studies in elite amateur boxers, no large meta-analysis yet]

A systematic review of 20 studies (504 athletes, 6 Olympic combat sports: judo, karate, boxing, fencing, wrestling — no taekwondo study met inclusion criteria) found strength training reliably improves maximal dynamic/isometric strength, power, flexibility, balance, and sport-specific actions. However, methodological quality was weak (average 45% on the TESTEX scale, only 2/20 studies scored above 50%), no effect sizes were poolable, and female athletes were almost entirely absent (1 study). [MODERATE quality of the underlying finding, LOW quality of the evidence base itself — treat magnitude claims cautiously]

Variable resistance training (VRT, bands/chains added to barbell work) improved max strength, vertical jump, and punch performance in elite amateur boxers in an RCT; 35% VRT favored jump/punch speed, 20% VRT favored punch force. Lower-core resistance training added to normal combat training improved cross-punch impact power and 5RM half-squat strength. [MODERATE — single RCTs, not yet replicated broadly]

### 2. Power / RFD / ballistic & plyometric training
Only resistance training and ballistic training reliably improve rate of force development (RFD) in trained athletes; the *intent* to move fast against the load — not the actual bar speed — drives the neural adaptations (motor unit recruitment, RFD, inter/intramuscular coordination). Loads of ~30-60% 1RM are the conventional target zone for RFD/ballistic work, vs 80-95% 1RM for maximal-strength adaptation. [PRACTICE-BASED to MODERATE — well-established RFD principle from general strength literature, not combat-specific RCTs]

A 2023 systematic review with meta-analysis (12 studies, 292 combat athletes — mostly taekwondo, silat, wrestling, judo, fencing, karate; median age <18) found plyometric-jump training (PJT), 4-12 weeks, 2-3 sessions/week, produced small-to-moderate improvements in maximal strength (Hedges g 0.77-1.04), CMJ (0.69-0.89), and change-of-direction speed (0.72), with only trivial-to-small transfer to "sport-specific performance" (0.35-0.72) and no effect on body composition. GRADE certainty was rated low-to-moderate. Intensity/box height was poorly reported across studies (drop-jump heights 15-50cm), and 9 of 12 studies were fencing/taekwondo — striking-sport (boxing/Muay Thai/kickboxing/MMA) generalizability is genuinely uncertain. [MODERATE for the general combat-sport-athlete population studied; WEAK specifically for boxing/MMA/kickboxing, since those sports were barely represented]

### 3. Speed — limb velocity / punch speed
Straight and reverse punches are expected to reach >10 m/s in trained boxers; the highest recorded mean punch force in the literature is ~3427 N for a straight punch. Lower-limb strength and power are consistently identified as key contributors to punch velocity and force (not just upper-body training) — punching is a ground-up kinetic-chain event, not an arm movement. [MODERATE — consistent biomechanical/correlational findings across a meta-analysis of upper-limb strike force/velocity and multiple wearable-sensor studies]

Reactive agility (movement in response to an unpredictable stimulus) and pre-planned change-of-direction speed (COD) are separate, largely independent motor qualities with low correlation between tests of each — meaning training one does not reliably transfer to the other. Reactive-agility-specific training (with a perceptual/decision-making component) in team-sport athletes produced ~3-6% gains over 6 weeks; COD drills alone do not automatically produce this. [MODERATE — established in team-sport literature; direct combat-sport RCTs are sparse, so application here is extrapolated]

### 4. Aerobic capacity & between-round recovery
Elite boxers show very high aerobic capacity (mean VO2max ~64 ml/kg/min, up to 69 in an Olympic medalist). In MMA, VO2max ≥53 ml/kg/min is associated with higher work output (Playerload) and lower perceived exertion during live sparring. Boxers' oxygen uptake during punching climbs from ~85% VO2max in round 1 to nearly 100% VO2max by the final round — meaning the aerobic system is what's left recovering you between exchanges as the fight wears on, even though individual actions are anaerobic. Aerobic fitness is explicitly identified as the primary determinant of between-round and within-round recovery capacity (lactate clearance, glycogen sparing, HR recovery). [STRONG for the physiological description; MODERATE for the specific claim that raising aerobic base is what most improves recovery, since most supporting studies are observational/correlational rather than controlled training interventions]

### 5. Anaerobic/glycolytic conditioning & lactate tolerance
Combat sports are intermittent — short high-intensity bursts (attacks, exchanges) interspersed with lower-intensity work, with the glycolytic system dominant for efforts up to roughly 2-3 minutes. Glycolytic/anaerobic training upregulates glycolytic enzyme activity, and elite athletes show a training effect over a competitive season: for example, judoka showed lower blood lactate for the same Wingate output closer to competition, indicating improved efficiency (lower glycolytic cost) rather than just higher lactate tolerance per se. [MODERATE — mechanistic/enzymatic evidence solid from general exercise physiology; combat-specific longitudinal data thinner]

### 6. Repeat-effort ability / HIIT / RSA
Two major systematic reviews with meta-analysis on HIIT in combat sports exist: Franchini, Cormack & Takito (2019, JSCR) on Olympic combat sports, and Vasconcelos et al. (2020, JSCR) with pooled meta-analysis. HIIT protocols produced max/peak aerobic performance gains of 4.4-23.0% with no significant change in body-fat percentage; a related meta-analysis found HIIT also improved lower-limb power, agility, and speed in martial artists. Repeat-sprint ability (RSA) — successive high-intensity efforts with short recovery, maintaining output — is described as more predictive of combat performance than a single top-speed number, since a 5-minute MMA round is roughly 30% high-intensity actions, 40% moderate-intensity/positional, 30% low-intensity. Individualized HIIT prescription (using measured physiological/time-motion data) outperforms generic protocols. [MODERATE-STRONG — two independent systematic reviews with meta-analysis converge; specific work:rest prescriptions are still practice-based]

### 7. Core / rotational-anti-rotational strength & kinetic-chain transfer
Trunk/core mass and strength show a measurable positive contribution to punch force in at least one study using medicine-ball punch-distance as a proxy, consistent with the mechanistic model that the core transmits ground-reaction force from the legs through the trunk into the strike ("effective mass" — the whole body stiffening at impact, not just arm speed). Anti-rotation (resisting an external rotational force, e.g., Pallof press) and rotational (producing rotational force, e.g., med-ball rotational throws) are described as distinct qualities that should both be trained; anti-rotational capacity is theorized to protect the shoulder/elbow/lumbar spine from overuse by improving force transfer efficiency, but this injury-protection claim is largely mechanistic/expert reasoning rather than RCT-verified in combat athletes specifically. [WEAK-MODERATE — the "core matters for punch power" claim has real evidence; the specific "anti-rotation vs. rotation, which exercise variant matters" prescriptions are PRACTICE-BASED, drawn mostly from golf/baseball/tennis literature and coach consensus, not combat-sport RCTs]

### 8. Mobility — hips, ankles, shoulders, thoracic spine
No RCTs specific to combat-sport mobility training and performance/injury outcomes surfaced in this search; the evidence here is predominantly descriptive/anatomical and expert-consensus. Kicking (roundhouse-style) requires hip internal/external rotation, ankle stability, core bracing and single-leg balance simultaneously — insufficient hip mobility is cited as a mechanism for femoroacetabular impingement risk in kicking athletes. Insufficient glenohumeral (shoulder) range is described as forcing compensatory thoracic extension, creating a "mechanical disadvantage" and elevated shoulder-injury risk, particularly relevant to clinch/ground exchanges. Injury patterns differ meaningfully by discipline: karate → lower back/hip/ankle; taekwondo → hip/ankle; judo → neck/shoulder/elbow/ankle; boxing → shoulder/neck/wrist; wrestling → ankle/shoulder/fingers — meaning a single generic mobility block is a worse design than discipline-aware emphasis. [PRACTICE-BASED — plausible mechanistic reasoning and injury-pattern epidemiology, but no combat-specific mobility-intervention RCTs found in this search]

On static stretching specifically: the old blanket "never static stretch before performance" rule is now considered an overcorrection. Short-duration static stretching (≤60 sec/muscle group) embedded within a full warm-up (aerobic + dynamic + sport-specific movement) produces only trivial (1-2%) power/strength decrements; only stretching beyond 60 seconds per muscle group produces meaningful (4-7.5%) impairment. Dynamic warm-ups remain the preferred default for pre-performance, but brief static stretching is not the performance killer it's often portrayed as. [STRONG — this is now a well-replicated finding in the general strength/power literature, not combat-specific but broadly applicable]

### 9. Concurrent training — interference effect & sequencing
The interference-effect literature has meaningfully softened since the original 2012 meta-analysis (which found concurrent training reduced strength/hypertrophy/power gains vs. resistance training alone). A large meta-analysis (Schumann et al., 43 studies) found that, across sequencing orders, aerobic modality, frequency, training status and age, concurrent training did **not** meaningfully interfere with hypertrophy or maximal strength. The moderating variables that matter are the **frequency and duration of the endurance component**: high-frequency, long-duration endurance work is what drives interference, not concurrent training per se. Practical guidance from combat-sport S&C sources: sequence strength before conditioning within a session, separate high-intensity sessions by type where possible, and use undulating periodization (e.g., heavy strength one day, power day, conditioning day) rather than stacking everything together; during fight camp, strength volume should be cut (1-2 short maintenance sessions/week) as skill/sparring volume rises, while intensity is held rather than dropped, and taper structure is training volume −40 to −60%, frequency held near 80%, intensity held until late, with sparring cut to once weekly in the final week and stopped 5-7 days out. [MODERATE for the general interference-effect findings — multiple recent meta-analyses converge; PRACTICE-BASED for the specific combat-sport sequencing/taper numbers, which come from coaching sources, not controlled trials in fighters]

## Concrete Recommendations for THE PROTOCOL

**Strength category:** Prioritize lower-body maximal strength (squat/hinge/lunge patterns, or at-home equivalents — single-leg/plyo-loaded bodyweight progressions, weighted-vest or resistance-band variants) as the primary strength driver of striking force, not upper-body pressing volume. Frame upper-body strength work as support/injury-resilience, not the main lever for punch power — this directly contradicts the common "big bench = big punch" belief and should be stated explicitly in user-facing copy. Include trunk/core loading (anti-rotation holds plus rotational power throws) as a distinct sub-block, not an afterthought — both qualities, not just one.

**Power category:** Use ballistic/plyometric movements (jumps, med-ball throws, explosive push-ups) at 2-3 sessions/week, with technique/landing quality gated before intensity is increased — the evidence-supported dose is 4-12 week blocks, 2-3x/week. Cue *intent to move fast* against light-to-moderate loads or bodyweight, not just heavy grinding reps. Be honest in the product that transfer from generic plyometrics to actual "sport-specific" striking performance is only small-to-trivial in the current evidence (Hedges g ~0.35-0.72) — position plyometrics as one contributor among several, not a magic-bullet power builder.

**Speed category:** Build lower-body power work into speed programming (it drives punch/kick velocity more than isolated arm-speed drills). Separate reactive-agility work (unpredictable stimulus, e.g., partner-called direction changes, light/app-based reaction cues) from pure change-of-direction drills — they are different qualities and the product should train both, not assume one covers the other.

**Conditioning (aerobic) category:** Include a genuine aerobic-base component (steady-state or long-interval work) as the physiological foundation for between-round recovery, not just high-intensity finishers — this is a mechanism the audience under-appreciates, since "getting gassed" is usually framed as an anaerobic problem when the recovery capacity between bursts is aerobic.

**Conditioning (anaerobic/RSA) category:** Use HIIT-style and repeat-effort interval formats (short high-intensity work with incomplete recovery, mimicking round structure) as the primary glycolytic-conditioning tool — this has the strongest evidence base of any conditioning modality reviewed here (two independent systematic reviews with meta-analysis, convergent findings, real effect sizes). Favor combat-specific movement patterns in these intervals (bag work, footwork circuits) over generic exercises where possible, since specificity improved outcomes in the studies reviewed.

**Mobility category:** Make mobility discipline-aware rather than one-size-fits-all if the product differentiates boxing/Muay Thai/kickboxing/MMA users — hip/ankle emphasis for kickers, shoulder/thoracic emphasis for boxers and grapplers. Use dynamic warm-ups as the default pre-training mobility approach; do not tell users static stretching before training is dangerous — that claim is now overstated relative to current evidence when duration is kept short.

**Sequencing/programming:** Default program structure should sequence strength before conditioning within a session, and should reduce strength-training volume (not necessarily intensity) as sparring/technical volume rises in a fight-camp-style block, consistent with both the concurrent-training and combat-coaching literature.

## Uncertainties & Disagreements

- **Plyometrics' real transfer to striking-specific sports (boxing/Muay Thai/kickboxing/MMA) is genuinely unknown.** The only combat-sport plyometric meta-analysis available is dominated by fencing and taekwondo (9 of 12 studies); THE PROTOCOL's target sports are barely represented in the underlying evidence.
- **No effect sizes exist for the broader Olympic combat-sports strength-training systematic review** (PMC9961120) — it establishes *direction* of effect (strength training helps) but not *magnitude*, and study quality was weak (avg. 45% on TESTEX).
- **Female combat athletes are almost absent from this evidence base** across nearly every review found (1 of 20 studies in the Olympic-sports strength review; 3 of 12 in the plyometric review). Any dose/frequency numbers should be treated as derived largely from male samples.
- **Mobility-specific intervention trials in combat athletes were not found in this search.** The mobility section here rests on injury-pattern epidemiology and biomechanical reasoning, not controlled trials showing that a mobility program changes performance or injury rates in fighters. This is a real gap, not just a search-coverage limitation — it should be flagged to the product team as an area where THE PROTOCOL's claims must stay conservative.
- **Concurrent-training interference-effect conclusions have shifted meaningfully in the last decade** (from "real and sizable" in 2012 to "minimal under most real-world conditions" in more recent large meta-analyses). Older combat-S&C content still circulating in the coaching world may overstate the risk of combining strength and conditioning work.
- **Exact work:rest ratios and interval durations for combat-specific HIIT are not standardized** across the reviewed literature — the two major meta-analyses report outcome ranges (4.4-23% aerobic improvement) without converging on one "best" protocol; individualized prescription based on measured fitness is repeatedly recommended over a fixed formula.

## What This Means For The Product

THE PROTOCOL can confidently tell users that leg/hip strength and power matter more for punch power than they probably think, that trunk/core work is a real contributor (not filler), that repeat-effort interval conditioning is the best-evidenced conditioning tool available, and that a big aerobic base is what actually lets them recover between exchanges late in a fight — all four are strongly supported and differentiate the product from generic "6-pack abs and heavy bench" fitness content. The plyometrics and mobility categories should be programmed with more epistemic humility: include them because the mechanistic/practice-based case is sound and downside risk is low, but avoid quantified performance-transfer promises ("plyometrics will increase your kick power by X%") since the direct evidence doesn't support a specific number, especially for striking sports. Programming logic (sequencing strength before conditioning, cutting strength volume as sparring ramps up) has real support and can be built into the app's periodization logic with reasonable confidence, while acknowledging the specific numbers (e.g., "-40 to -60% volume in taper") come from coaching practice, not RCTs, and should be presented as sensible defaults, not proven optima.

## Claims To Avoid

- Do NOT claim "heavy bench press / push-ups build your punch power" as a primary mechanism — the evidence specifically shows upper-body maximal strength does *not* differentiate high- vs. low-impact punchers; lower-body strength does. Avoid marketing copy that implies upper-body strength training is the main lever for striking power.
- Do NOT promise a specific quantified performance gain from plyometric/jump training on striking-sport performance (e.g., "increase your punch power by X%") — the only combat-sport meta-analysis shows only trivial-to-small transfer to sport-specific performance, and it's barely tested in striking sports specifically.
- Do NOT tell users static stretching before training will hurt their power output — that blanket claim is outdated; only stretching beyond ~60 seconds per muscle group shows meaningful impairment.
- Do NOT frame the interference effect ("cardio kills your gains") as settled fact for a general audience doing moderate volumes — current meta-analytic evidence shows minimal interference under realistic training frequencies/durations; overstating this could push users toward under-programming conditioning.
- Do NOT present any specific mobility protocol as evidence-proven to reduce injury or boost performance in combat athletes — no controlled trials were found; frame mobility work as sound practice/anatomical reasoning, not as an evidence-backed guarantee.
- Do NOT cite single-study findings (e.g., one VRT boxing RCT, one lower-core RCT) as established consensus — they are promising but not yet replicated; present them as "early evidence suggests," not "research proves."
- Do NOT apply findings from taekwondo/fencing-heavy plyometric research, or team-sport reactive-agility research, directly to boxing/Muay Thai/MMA claims without the caveat that direct evidence in those specific sports is thinner.
