# THE PROTOCOL — Research Track 2: At-Home / Minimal-Equipment Programming

Scope: what can genuinely be trained to a high standard with bodyweight / minimal equipment / no equipment, across Power, Speed, Conditioning, Footwork, Strength, Mobility — and where it structurally cannot. Evidence gathered via ~24 web searches against systematic reviews, meta-analyses, and NSCA/sports-science sources. Every key claim is graded [STRONG] / [MODERATE] / [WEAK] / [PRACTICE-BASED].

---

## Key Findings

1. **Hypertrophy is largely load-independent; maximal strength is not.** Across loading ranges, similar muscle growth occurs whether training near failure with light loads or moderate loads with heavier weight — but maximal (1RM-type) strength gains are consistently superior with heavy loads (>60% 1RM). [STRONG] This is the single most important fact for product design: bodyweight training can build real muscle and real work capacity, but it has a genuine, physics-limited ceiling on maximal force output once bodyweight-only exercises stop getting harder to load.

2. **Training to failure is not required for hypertrophy or strength gains**, and evidence for its superiority is trivial-to-mixed once volume is equated. [STRONG] This matters for bodyweight training because it means fighters don't need externally-loaded failure sets to progress — proximity to failure (RIR 0–3) with harder bodyweight variations works.

3. **Calisthenics and matched-load resistance training produce statistically similar hypertrophy and strength gains** when volume/intensity are equated (push-up vs. bench press studies at matched EMG/load). [MODERATE-STRONG] But this equivalence breaks down at higher absolute strength levels, because bodyweight exercises run out of headroom before a barbell does.

4. **Isometrics are a legitimate, evidence-backed substitute for external load**, particularly for tendon stiffness, joint-angle-specific strength, and rate of force development — but adaptations are highly joint-angle-specific and don't transfer well across the full range of motion unless paired with dynamic work. [STRONG]

5. **Plyometric/jump training has established weekly foot-contact volume guidelines** (roughly 80–120 contacts/session for intermediates, up to 120–200 for advanced, 1–2 sessions/week) that translate directly into apartment-safe programming with low-impact substitutions. [MODERATE-STRONG]

6. **Unilateral (single-leg) training is not simply "half of bilateral"** — it produces its own specific adaptations (unilateral strength, change-of-direction, asymmetry correction) that don't fully transfer to bilateral bar-loaded lifts, and vice versa. [MODERATE] This is good news for at-home programming, since single-leg work (split squats, step-ups, Bulgarian split squats, Nordics) is one of the strongest tools available without a barbell.

7. **Nordic hamstring curls, done bodyweight-only, are among the best-evidenced injury-prevention/strength exercises in all of sports science** — historically cited as ~50% reduction in hamstring strain risk, though a later methodological reappraisal weakened confidence in the exact magnitude. [MODERATE, downgraded from STRONG per reappraisal] This is a case where minimal equipment (a partner, a bar, or a strap under a couch) matches or beats gym-based alternatives.

8. **Resistance bands and elastic training produce comparable strength gains to free weights** in untrained-to-moderately-trained populations, with the caveat that bands lose tension at end-range and provide minimal eccentric overload — a real limitation for a fighter chasing eccentric/deceleration strength. [MODERATE-STRONG]

9. **Dynamic stretching pre-training improves performance; static stretching pre-training acutely reduces strength/power** (~5% strength, ~2-3% power) but is valuable post-training or in dedicated sessions for durable range-of-motion gains. [STRONG]

10. **Static stretching and resistance training produce statistically similar range-of-motion improvements** — meaning strength training through full range (loaded stretching / end-range training) can double as flexibility work, which is efficient for a minimal-equipment context. [MODERATE]

## What Can Be Trained Well At Home

**Muscular endurance and moderate hypertrophy (all six categories, especially upper body, core, posterior chain).** Push-up, pull-up, dip, split squat, and Nordic-family progressions, taken close to failure with tempo/eccentric manipulation, produce hypertrophy on par with matched-load gym training. [STRONG — load-independence of hypertrophy] This directly serves Power (build the engine), Conditioning, and general work capacity.

**Single-leg / unilateral lower-body strength.** Bulgarian split squats, rear-foot-elevated split squats, step-ups, reverse lunges, single-leg RDLs, and Nordic curls replicate much of what a barbell squat or leg curl machine offers, and in some cases (unilateral strength, asymmetry correction, change-of-direction) exceed bilateral barbell training in specificity to a fighter's actual movement patterns (most striking, clinching, and footwork loads one leg at a time). [MODERATE-STRONG] A single sturdy chair/bench/step is the only "equipment" required.

**Isometric strength and tendon resilience.** Overcoming isometrics (pressing into an immovable object — a doorframe, a partner, the floor) build genuine rate-of-force-development and neural drive; long-duration yielding isometrics (wall sits, extended plank/split-squat holds, 30+ second holds at ≥70% effort) build tendon stiffness that is directly protective against the tendinopathies common in kicking/punching sports. [STRONG] This is arguably the single best-kept secret of at-home training — it needs a wall, a doorframe, or a partner and nothing else.

**Plyometric power development, dosed correctly.** Foot-contact volume (not exercise selection) is the governing variable — 80–120 quality contacts per session, 1–2x/week, is enough to drive real jump/power adaptations. Low-impact substitutions (pogo hops instead of depth jumps, squat-to-calf-raise instead of squat jump, banded/med-ball-free rotational throws using a towel or light object) keep this apartment-viable. [MODERATE-STRONG]

**Conditioning, including genuine high-intensity work.** Shadowboxing intervals, skipping rope, and stair sprints are validated HIIT/conditioning modalities producing measurable VO2max and cardiorespiratory improvements — brief (10-min/day) stair-climbing protocols alone raised VO2peak ~12% in 6 weeks in one RCT. [MODERATE-STRONG] Jump rope produces comparable caloric/aerobic demand to running at matched intensity, with the added benefit of coordination and calf/tendon stiffness work baked in. [MODERATE]

**Core strength and anti-rotation.** Pallof press (band or towel-resisted), side planks, bird dogs, and mountain climbers all have EMG-supported activation of the deep stabilizers a fighter needs for rotational power transfer and injury resistance, and require no equipment beyond an optional light band. [MODERATE]

**Footwork, coordination, and movement quality.** These are almost entirely equipment-free by nature — agility-ladder patterns (tape on a floor works identically to a real ladder), shadowboxing pivots/angles, and cone drills (use shoes/books as markers) all fit in a few square meters. [PRACTICE-BASED, low research volume but high face-validity and near-zero downside]

**Mobility — most of it.** Dynamic warm-up stretching pre-training, static stretching post-training, and loaded/end-range strength work (deep split-squat holds, deep lunge isometrics) durably improve range of motion and, per direct comparison, work about as well as dedicated stretching protocols. [STRONG for timing rules; MODERATE for loaded-stretching equivalence]

## What Cannot (and Honest Alternatives)

**Maximal absolute strength and heavy-load hypertrophy for the largest muscle groups (posterior chain, quads under axial load, deadlift-pattern strength) has a real ceiling with bodyweight-only training.** [STRONG] A barbell squat or deadlift can load past 200% of bodyweight; a pistol squat or single-leg RDL cannot exceed ~100% of bodyweight (the trainee's own mass) without external load. Elite weighted-calisthenics athletes prove very high *relative* strength is achievable, but this takes years of skill-intensive progression (levers, planches, one-arm work) that is a poor use of a fighter's limited training time and carries real injury risk from the joint stress of advanced calisthenics skill work. **Alternative:** treat bodyweight/unilateral training as the base layer, and flag clearly to users that a single piece of external load (a heavy backpack, a resistance band anchored for split squats, one adjustable kettlebell/dumbbell, or a loaded carry object) removes 80% of this ceiling for a few dollars. This is not "calisthenics replaces the barbell" — it does not, and the product should say so.

**True maximal-velocity / heavy-eccentric-overload training (the kind that most reliably builds sprint- and punch-specific rate of force development) is hard to replicate without falling weight.** Tempo and eccentric-emphasis bodyweight work (slow negatives, single-leg lowering) helps, but cannot match a loaded barbell eccentric or a weighted jump-and-stick for peak force. [MODERATE] **Alternative:** heavy carries, weighted vest work if available, or accepting that peak absolute power output plateaus earlier at home than in a full gym — and being honest with users about that plateau rather than overselling bodyweight plyometrics as a full substitute.

**Resistance bands lose tension at end-range and cannot overload the eccentric phase**, which limits their value for the deceleration/eccentric strength that protects joints in striking sports. [MODERATE] **Alternative:** pair bands with slow-eccentric bodyweight work, or use bands mainly for the concentric/power end of movements rather than as a full free-weight replacement.

**Loud, high-impact plyometrics (depth jumps, box jumps, heavy skipping on hard floors) are genuinely constrained in apartments/hotel rooms by noise and downstairs neighbors, not just by choice.** [PRACTICE-BASED] **Alternative:** the low-impact substitution list (pogo hops, squat-to-calf-raise, lateral floor runs, toe-to-ball-of-foot landings) is a real, research-informed workaround, not a compromise — but true depth-jump-level eccentric shock training (needed for elite reactive strength) is not fully replicable quietly indoors. Users chasing elite-level reactive power need occasional access to a park, gym, or outdoor surface.

**VO2max ceiling work.** Jump rope and shadowboxing intervals are good, validated conditioning tools, but the single best-evidenced modality for raw VO2max development remains running/rowing/cycling at sustained high intensity; jump rope research shows comparable but not clearly superior aerobic adaptation. [MODERATE] **Alternative:** stair sprints (validated, minimal equipment, apartment/hotel-viable) are the closest true substitute when road-running access is limited.

**Isometric range-of-motion transfer.** Isometric strength gains are sharply joint-angle-specific — training a deep lunge hold at one angle does not fully strengthen the whole range. [STRONG] **Alternative:** isometrics must be programmed across multiple joint angles (e.g., three lunge depths, not one) or paired with dynamic reps to cover full ROM — a programming detail the product needs to get right rather than "add an isometric hold" as a token gesture.

## Exercise Library Recommendations By Quality Category

**Power** — Plyometric progressions dosed by foot contact (pogo hops → bounds → depth-jump substitutes outdoors), overcoming isometrics with maximal intent, medicine-ball-free rotational throws (light bag/towel), Nordic curl eccentrics for posterior-chain power transfer.

**Speed** — Resisted starts using a partner/band, footwork ladder/tape drills, reactive shadowboxing with called combinations, short hill/stair sprints outdoors.

**Conditioning** — Shadowboxing rounds (3-min work/1-min rest structure mirrors combat sport format), skipping rope intervals, stair-climbing HIIT (validated protocol: short vigorous bouts, ~20–30s, with 2 min recovery), bodyweight circuit intervals (burpee-family, mountain climbers) — intensity governed by RPE/talk-test since no HR monitor is assumed.

**Footwork** — Agility-ladder/tape patterns, shadowboxing pivot-and-angle drills, mirror-work for visual feedback, cone/marker drills using household objects.

**Strength** — Split squat family (Bulgarian, rear-foot-elevated, reverse lunge) progressed by tempo/single-leg-load/range before adding external load; Nordic curl progressions; push-up/dip/pull-up progressions to failure or near-failure; loaded carries with any single heavy household object (backpack, water jugs, sandbag) for total-body strength-endurance and grip.

**Mobility** — Dynamic movement-prep pre-training; static stretching post-training; loaded end-range holds (deep split squat, deep lunge, Nordic-lowering) as dual-purpose strength+ROM work; isometric holds programmed across 2–3 joint angles per movement.

## Space & Noise Constraints

- **Bedroom/small space:** isometrics, core work, mobility, shadowboxing (no ground-contact impact), tempo-based strength work — all fully viable in a 2m x 2m footprint.
- **Apartment with downstairs neighbors:** low-impact plyometric substitutions (pogo hops on forefoot, squat-to-calf-raise, lateral floor runs), jump rope done low-to-ground with soft toe-to-ball landing, moving to room center (fewer floor squeaks/resonance), barefoot or cushioned-surface landing to force lighter footfall. True depth jumps and heavy box work should be flagged as "needs outdoor/gym access."
- **Hotel room:** identical constraints to apartment; add isometrics and band work as the highest-value, zero-noise options; carpet changes impact profile favorably.
- **Park/outdoor:** unlocks true plyometric shock training, sprint work, stair sprints, and loaded carries at higher intensity than any indoor substitute — the product should route "true power ceiling" work here when available rather than pretend indoor substitutes are equivalent.

## Minimal-Equipment Value Ranking (best value-per-dollar for a fighter)

1. **Skipping rope (~$8–15)** — validated conditioning + coordination + calf/tendon stiffness tool in one, near-zero cost, works in almost any space with enough ceiling height.
2. **Resistance bands / loop bands (~$12–20 for a set)** — comparable strength gains to free weights for untrained-to-intermediate lifters, adds load to split squats, presses, rows, and Pallof-press core work; main gap is eccentric/end-range tension.
3. **Doorway or freestanding pull-up bar (~$25–40)** — the one bodyweight-strength category (vertical pulling) that has no good bodyweight-only substitute; direct, load-bearing, and irreplaceable for back/grip/pull strength.
4. **Suspension trainer (TRX-style, ~$40–60)** — strong core-activation and adjustable-leverage strength tool, RCT-supported strength gains in 8–12 weeks, doubles as a portable travel gym.
5. **One adjustable kettlebell or a pair of adjustable dumbbells (~$50–100+)** — the single highest-leverage purchase for breaking the bodyweight strength ceiling described above; unlocks loaded carries, goblet squats, presses, and true progressive overload.
6. **Weighted vest (~$40–80, optional/later-stage)** — extends bodyweight strength progressions (push-ups, pull-ups, split squats) past bodyweight-only ceiling without needing a full equipment stack.

## Uncertainties

- Direct head-to-head trials of "bodyweight-only program" vs. "full barbell program" in trained combat-sport athletes specifically (rather than general trainees) were not found — most load-independence findings come from general resistance-training literature, and combat-sport-specific S&C position statements from NSCA/UKSCA proved hard to locate as dedicated documents (searches returned adjacent youth/weightlifting position stands, not a combat-sports-specific one).
- The magnitude of the Nordic curl injury-prevention effect (the often-cited "50% reduction") has been challenged by a methodological reappraisal; treat the effect direction as credible but the exact magnitude as contested.
- Research on step-ups' transfer to actual sport performance is thin — mostly older (early 1990s) studies; current recommendations rest more on biomechanical plausibility and EMG data than on outcome trials.
- Talk-test and RPE validity specifically for *unsupervised home* training (no coach, no lab) has not been systematically studied — the validity evidence largely comes from supervised/clinical settings and may not fully generalize to a solo user following an app.
- No studies were found directly testing "quiet/low-impact plyometric substitutions" (pogo hops, squat-to-calf-raise) against standard plyometrics for actual power transfer — the noise-reduction guidance is well-supported for noise itself but the substitutes' training-effect equivalence is inferred, not directly tested.
- Search access was not interrupted and no rate-limit was hit; all ~24 planned searches completed. Given time/token constraints, this report synthesizes search-result summaries rather than fetching and reading full-text PDFs of every cited study — spot-checking primary sources before publishing exact effect sizes (e.g., the "5.4% strength reduction," "12% VO2peak increase") is recommended if those numbers will be quoted verbatim in user-facing content.

## What This Means For The Product

The Protocol can credibly promise real, evidence-backed progress across all six categories using bodyweight and under-$150 of equipment — this is not a compromise product, it is a legitimately different but valid training system, provided programming respects three rules: (1) push proximity-to-failure and progression through harder variations, tempo, and unilateral loading rather than assuming reps alone drive adaptation; (2) use isometrics deliberately and multi-angle, not as a token "hold this" filler; (3) dose plyometric/impact work by foot-contact volume, not vibes, with a clear low-impact swap library for noise-constrained users.

The one place the product must not oversell is maximal absolute strength and heavy posterior-chain hypertrophy — the honest, user-facing message should be: "this system will make you strong, explosive, and durable; if you want to keep pushing strength past what your bodyweight can provide, the single best dollar-for-dollar upgrade is one adjustable kettlebell/dumbbell or a resistance band set — not a full gym membership." This keeps the product's credibility intact with any user who also trains at a gym, and gives a natural, evidence-based upsell/equipment-recommendation path (ranked list above) rather than a vague "buy some equipment" gesture.

Space/noise constraints are a real design input, not an edge case — a meaningful share of the target user base is training in apartments, hotel rooms, or shared housing, and the product should treat "quiet mode" / low-impact substitution as a first-class programming toggle, not an afterthought footnote.
